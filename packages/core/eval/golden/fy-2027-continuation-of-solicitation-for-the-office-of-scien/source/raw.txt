Before You Begin
Navigating the Notice of Funding Opportunity
To reduce the burden on applicants in the Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO)
process and limit the length of the NOFO information requests, we separated the NOFO into two
parts.
The NOFO Part 1 describes the Department of Energy (DOE) program goals and
evaluation criteria, eligibility, and other components specific to each funding opportunity.
NOFO Part 2 includes the fixed DOE requirements that generally do not change from NOFO to
NOFO, including standard information for the application phase, expectations for award
negotiations, and post-award requirements. You must review both parts before applying. To
assist you in the process, you will find references throughout this document to additional
information in Part 2.
You must also take several one-time actions before applying. Some of these actions may
take several weeks, so be sure to allow yourself enough time to complete them. If you don't
complete all required steps, it could interfere with application and negotiation deadlines or your
ability to receive an award if selected. If you already completed these one-time registrations,
make sure they are active and up to date. All registrations are free. You can find additional
information about registration in NOFO Part 2, Get Registered.
Hyperlinks are provided for the applicant's ease of reference. Links, and the information
contained in them, may change over time. It is up to an individual applicant to make sure that
they have the current information that is contained in the links.
This announcement is published with NOFO Part 2 Version 4.1. Part 2 will be posted
with this NOFO at https://pamspublic.science.energy.gov, https://grants.gov, and
https://science.osti.gov/grants/FOAs/open.

1. Advanced Scientific Computing Research (ASCR)
Program website: https://science.osti.gov/ascr
The mission of the Advanced Scientific Computing Research (ASCR) program is to
advance applied mathematics and computer science, including artificial intelligence (AI) and
quantum information science (QIS); deliver the most sophisticated computational scientific
applications in partnership with disciplinary science; create first-of-a-kind advanced computing
and networking capabilities for the Nation; and develop future generations of computing
hardware and software tools for science and engineering in partnership with the research
community, including U.S. industry.
ASCR's research and facilities investments increase the capability, versatility, and
efficiency of scientific computing through activities described by four thrusts.
Breakthrough Tools and Technologies: ASCR enhances software, data processes, and AI
for increasingly complex or resource intense modeling and simulation, including enabling
the convergence of AI with QIS.
Deep Understanding of AI and Physical Models: ASCR advances knowledge in core
mathematical methods, algorithms, and data visualization and analytics that underlie all
AI, scientific modelling, and numerical simulation.
Enabling High-precision R&D: ASCR focuses on concurrently advancing applied math
and computer science knowledge with disciplinary science in critical areas such as fusion
energy and material science.
Hardware Innovation: ASCR increases the robustness of computing, including
underlying communication and energy needs, redefines the art of possible in conventional
computing, and leads the development of new emerging technologies.
ASCR supports cross-disciplinary research where domains of scientific inquiry may
provide problems and data that provide use-cases for computer scientists and applied
mathematicians to devise generalized methods, models, algorithms and tools. ASCR's interest in
these fields is not to solve the specific problems in other scientific domains but to use those
challenges to advance the state of the art and increase knowledge in its fields of research. ASCR
advances key areas of computational science and discovery that support the missions of SC
through mutually beneficial partnerships.
The computing resources and high-speed networks required to meet SC needs exceed the
state-of-the-art by a significant margin. To establish and maintain DOE's leadership in mission-
focused scientific areas, ASCR operates leadership computing facilities (Argonne Leadership

The ASCR program seeks research focused on innovation in applied mathematics and
computer science. ASCR's applied mathematics and computer science research is typically
generalizable to multiple applications, and successful applications often demonstrate such
generalizability. In addition, priority is often given to research and applications motivated in the
context of SC user facilities, especially those supported by ASCR. The ASCR subprograms and
their objectives follow.
(a) Applied Mathematics
Website: https://science.osti.gov/ascr/Research/Applied-Mathematics
This subprogram supports basic research leading to fundamental mathematical advances
and computational breakthroughs across DOE and SC missions. The subprogram has supported
basic research in novel deterministic or randomized numerical methods for the scalable solution
of large-scale, linear and nonlinear systems of equations, including those solution methods that
take into consideration the possibilities brought about by future high performance computing
architectures; optimization techniques and next-generation solvers; numerical methods for
modeling multiscale, multi-physics, or multi-component continuous or discrete systems that span
a wide range of time and length scales; methods of simulation and analysis of systems that
account for the uncertainties of the systems, or are inherently stochastic or uncertain; innovative
approaches for analyzing, extracting insight from, or reducing large-scale data sets; and
foundational research in scientific machine learning and AI as a cross-cutting area of interest for
enabling greater adaptivity, automation, and predictive capabilities in scientific computing. We
are looking for approaches that are efficient and scalable for problems of increasingly high
dimensionality and computational complexity and that take advantage of current and emerging
high-performance computing architectures or ecosystems.

Notice of Submission Requirements for the Panel Review on Algorithms for Large-Scale Sparse
Linear Algebra on Modern and Emerging Supercomputer Architectures
Sparse linear algebra underlies many large-scale DOE science applications, but
significant opportunities exist to increase the efficiency with which relevant algorithms utilize
the GPUs of modern supercomputers. ASCR seeks applications for novel algorithms for sparse
operations tailored either to such contemporary supercomputers or to emerging technologies
such as dataflow architectures, application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), or neuromorphic
hardware. Although algorithms must be aware of the hardware, the focus is on ideas that
contribute to applied mathematics; applications for research in hardware design, memory
management, runtime systems, etc., lie outside the scope of this panel.

(b) Computer Science
Website: https://science.osti.gov/ascr/Research/Computer-Science
This subprogram supports research that advances computing and networking at extreme
scales (i.e., exascale computing, massively parallelism, big data) and the understanding of
extreme scale, or complex data from both simulations and experiments. The subprogram aims to
make high performance scientific computers and networks highly productive and efficient to
solve scientific challenges while attempting to reduce domain science application complexity as
much as possible. The computer science program does this in the context of sharp increases in
the heterogeneity and complexity of computing systems; the need to integrate simulation, data
analysis, and other tasks seamlessly and intelligently into coherent and usable workflows; and in
the context of hardware, architecture, and workflows for highly novel computing platforms, such
as neuromorphic and quantum systems, and other next-generation computing platforms for
science.

Priority interests for the program include AI for science (e.g. federated algorithm,
interpretable and explainable AI, neuro-symbolic approaches); data analysis and visualization

environment (Basic Research Needs in The Science of Scientific Software Development and Use:
Investment in Software is Investment in Science).
The computer science subprogram is also interested in operating and runtime systems;
performance portability and co-design (Basic Research Needs for Reimagining Codesign for
Advanced Scientific Computing); scheduling and resource management; network-offloaded
acceleration for distributed/parallel computing; computer science and information theory for
thermodynamics efficiency; advances in memory technologies; and distributed memory
architectures and innovations in algorithms, software interfaces, programming languages to
effectively exploit new processing-in-memory architectures, memory safety, the smoothening of
the spectrum from volatile to non-volatile memories, and on-the-fly compression and
decompression strategies.

(d) Experimental Condensed Matter Physics
The Experimental Condensed Matter Physics (ECMP) subprogram supports research that

many-body quantum interactions extending far beyond single-particle descriptions. Although
remarkable advances have been made in optical, transport, scattering, and thermal

(f) Physical Behavior of Materials
The Physical Behavior of Materials (PBM) subprogram supports basic research that aims
to advance our fundamental understanding and control of the processes and interactions that
determine the physical behavior of materials. It supports studies of how materials respond to
external stimuli, such as temperature, pressure, electromagnetic fields, and strain. Also of
interest are the influence of defects, disorder, heterogeneity, surfaces, and interfaces on the
physical behavior of materials.
The subprogram emphasizes research on the structure-property relationships governing
the physical behavior of materials. This research includes understanding how atomic structure
and defects result in semiconducting, superconducting, and magnetic properties, as well as novel
diffusion and transport phenomena. Projects within the subprogram currently explore the
physical behavior of material systems such as two-dimensional materials and heterostructures,
spintronics and magnetics, plasmonics, nanophotonics, and other complex and disordered
systems. Key thematic research thrusts include: (1) transport phenomena (spin, thermal, and
charge), (2) influence of electromagnetic fields on material behavior, (3) quasiparticle derived
behavior, and (4) structure- and nanoscale-enabled behavior.

(m) Gas Phase Chemical Physics
Research in the Gas Phase Chemical Physics (GPCP) subprogram is pursued through
three primary thrust areas. These areas represent distinct yet complementary approaches to
understanding and controlling gas-phase chemical phenomena. The thrusts are: Fundamental
Chemical Reactivity, which focuses on elementary processes; Emergent Complexity and
Systems Chemistry, which addresses collective behaviors in large molecular systems and
ensembles; and Gas-Surface Chemical Physics, which targets surface-mediated reactions.
Applicants are encouraged to propose research that aligns with the core focus of one or more of
these thrusts.
Fundamental Chemical Reactivity
This area supports research aimed at establishing the fundamental principles of gas-phase
reactivity relevant to the BES energy mission. Experimental and theoretical investigations where
the primary objective is to yield generalizable insights holds the highest priority in this area.
This emphasis includes studies of chemical kinetics, reaction mechanisms, chemical dynamics,
and collisional energy transfer specifically designed to target prototypical molecular interactions
or critical processes governing reactivity. Similarly, the construction and calculation of
molecular potential energy surfaces must be explicitly coupled to modeling these dynamic and
reactive processes. This area encompasses electron-driven chemistry and encourages the
development and application of innovative tools, only when used to reveal transferable physical
principles of energy flow and chemical transformation in the gas phase.
Emergent Complexity and Systems Chemistry
This area focuses on understanding how complex, collective behaviors arise from
underlying elementary gas-phase reactions. Research in this area must aim to establish the
mechanistic pathways that connect elementary processes to the observed behavior of large
reaction networks and molecular ensembles. The goal is to understand why and predict when
emergent phenomena, such as significant changes in reaction rates, branching ratios, or product
distributions, will manifest. This research includes the study of gas-particle interconversion, with
a specific focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms of particle formation, growth, and
transformation. In this context, the subprogram supports research that uses AI/ML methods as a
tool to analyze and discover the fundamental principles governing the behavior of these
increasingly complex gas-phase systems.
Gas-Surface Chemical Physics
This area emphasizes molecular-scale investigations where the surface is used as a well-
characterized boundary to influence gas-phase phenomena. The goal is to develop a fundamental
understanding of the cooperative effects that arise from coupling of gas-phase chemistry with an
interface. To be responsive, research must answer fundamental questions about how gas-phase
reactivity, kinetics, or energy transfer are altered by proximity to the surface. Therefore, the
central scientific questions must be rooted in the gas-phase, not the intrinsic catalytic properties
of the condensed-phase material itself.

(r) Separation Science
This subprogram provides mechanistic and molecular insight into chemical separation
processes or phenomena by supporting fundamental, testable hypothesis-based experimental and
computational research questions. Research projects seek to develop foundational knowledge
that enable prediction and control of chemical and physical mass transport and phase de-mixing.
This research includes understanding the kinetics, thermodynamics, and interfacial dynamics of
these transitions across multiple length and time scales, from quantum interactions to
macroscopic phenomena. Although parameters such as selectivity, capacity, throughput,
durability, and energy input are important performance benchmarks, projects must not focus on
optimization. Instead, projects must focus on molecular-level insight and mechanistic
understanding into chemical separation processes or phenomena. The subprogram encourages
nascent stage separation science projects for energy-relevant streams (e.g., critical minerals and
materials, industrial gases or liquids such as methane) as well as projects that use AI to provide
fundamental insight in separation mechanisms.
Scientific research topics that align with current subprogram priorities are:
Kinetics and Chemical Conversions. Elucidating the temporal molecular mechanisms
governing separation system changes, including activation, degradation, or solvation.
Interfacial Dynamics. Understanding how dynamics and molecular criteria limit phase
and mass transfer at interfaces or interfacial regions during separation processes.
Overcoming Thermodynamics via Cooperative Chemical Interactions. Discovering and
predicting mechanistic paradigms for selective isolation and release of dilute constituents
from complex mixtures using cooperative chemical phenomena.
Under-Explored Physical Driving Forces. Exploring highly efficient, alternative
separation mechanisms, such as bio-inspired pathways, chirality, magnetic or magneto-
reactive forces, or other non-thermal inputs.

(s) Heavy Element Chemistry
This subprogram supports actinide and transactinide fundamental chemical research that
underpins the DOE missions in energy, environment, and national security with an emphasis on
the chemical and physical properties of the transuranic elements. The unique molecular bonding
of these elements is explored using experiment and theory to elucidate electronic and molecular
structure, reaction thermodynamics, as well as quantum phenomena.
The emphasis in this subprogram is placed on the chemical and physical properties of the
transuranic elements to determine their bonding and reactivity, the fundamental transactinide
chemical properties, and the overarching goal of resolving the f-electron challenge. The f-
electron challenge refers to the inadequacy of current electronic structure methods to accurately
describe the behavior of f-electrons, in particular strong correlation, spin-orbit coupling,
multiplet complexity, and associated relativistic effects. We will consider theoretical
applications that integrate with experimental research or otherwise demonstrate impact outside
the theory community. Transactinide element research is highly encouraged; these
investigations, where relativistic chemical effects dominate and the half-lives are short, are a
challenging test of theoretical and chemical techniques.

(c) Low Dose Radiation Research
The U.S. population is continuously exposed to low-dose and low-dose-rate ionizing
radiation (IR) from several different sources, including natural background sources, medical
applications, and occupational exposures. BER's Low Dose Radiation Research seeks to take
advantage of advanced model systems, high throughput genomics and other multi-omics
approaches, and computational AI/ML tools and technologies to advance a more mechanistic
understanding of low dose and low dose rate radiation effects on cellular metabolism. Over the
long-term, this approach seeks to provide a basis for identifying biomarkers and/or patterns of
altered cellular functions that may be indicate or be associated with an adverse health outcome.
Epidemiological studies of populations exposed to IR have generally focused on cancer,
however, increasing evidence shows that non-cancer health outcomes such as cardiovascular
disease, neurological disorders, and immune dysfunction are also possible. Integrated biological
and computational approaches may provide new insights into reducing the uncertainties of health
effects in epidemiological studies following low dose (≤100 mGy) and low dose rate (≤5
mGy/hr) exposures and provide a basis to explore links to IR-induced health effects.
BER is interested in applications for integrated biological and computational low dose
radiation research to gain further mechanistic understanding of the effects of low dose radiation
exposure on cellular functions. The long-term goal is to build on the understanding of IR effects
on cellular function and establish comprehensive datasets we can incorporate into increasingly
capable AI/ML models to identify both transient and persistent changes in cellular metabolism
and tissue states that may be linked to adverse health outcomes. This funding opportunity calls
for biological research applications conducted with well-characterized exposures confirmed by
physical dosimetry methods to low energy transfer (LET) IR sources (high energy electrons,
protons, X- and gamma rays) and high LET sources (alpha particles, neutrons) across a range of
low dose exposures (<100 mGy encompassing 10 mGy) and dose rate ranges encompassing 5
mGy/hr within a combination of the four focus areas below.

mechanisms of cellular response to radiation exposure and for extrapolating to potential adverse
outcomes in humans. This program is also interested in model systems of sufficient genetic

The innermost wall of a fusion device, known as the plasma-facing component, is

Structural materials refer to those materials used in the overall construction of the fusion

materials must be able to withstand the structural loads, radiation, and heat while maintaining
their desired material properties. Advanced alloys and composite materials are areas of interest
for research.
Fundamental Modeling and Materials Behavior
Advances in materials modeling play a crucial role in predicting how materials will
behave under fusion reactor conditions. Computational tools allow researchers to simulate
material responses to high temperatures, radiation, and stress, aiding in material selection and
design optimization.
Functional Materials
These materials impact the functionality of various fusion components beyond their
structural integrity. For instance, materials used for sensors and heat transfer enhancement fall
under this category. This category usually has cross-program application to the Fusion Nuclear
Science subprogram in FES and is typically submitted to that other subprogram.

(d) Compact Toroidal Concepts
The Compact Toroidal Concepts subprogram supports research needed to develop a
compact toroidal configuration. Two promising concepts are the spherical tokamak (ST), such
as the National Spherical Torus Experimental-Upgrade (NSTX-U), and conventional aspect ratio
tokamaks operated at high toroidal magnetic fields, exemplified by the SPARC tokamak.
These devices offer complementary strategies for improving confinement and achieving
compactness: STs use enhanced plasma physics properties while high-field conventional
tokamaks rely on high-field magnets. Regardless of the approach, enabling technologies are
essential for delivering these compact designs, including high-temperature superconducting
magnets and liquid metal plasma-facing components. With several private sector stakeholders
pushing the frontiers, the subprogram naturally incorporates the fusion energy industry and
fosters strong connections to foundational S&T research.
The primary scientific research topics for STs directly address the Roadmap Core
Challenge C1 S&T gap and corresponding metrics by using enhanced plasma physics properties

(j) Plasma Science and Technology — High Energy Density Physics
The HEDP subprogram seeks to understand how matter behaves under extreme
conditions of temperature and pressure. In this domain, energy density is typically defined as
being above 1 Mbar (100 GPa or approximately 1 million atmospheres), which is comparable to
the energy density of a chemical bond, such as in a water molecule. HEDP conditions are crucial
for studying processes such as the formation and destruction of stars and for controlling fusion in
laboratory settings. Topics of interest for this NOFO include but are not limited to the following
areas.
Relativistic HED Plasmas and Intense Beam Physics
This area includes relativistic laser and beam propagation, relativistic laser-solid
interactions, ultrahigh energy density plasmas at the quantum electrodynamic (QED) limit,
relativistic thermal plasmas, and relativistic shocks. Key questions include: How do plasmas
dominated by relativistic effects behave? What new collective behaviors and fundamental
physics can we uncover at the relativistic limits of high-energy density physics? Additionally,
how can we generate ultra-bright vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) and X-ray sources that surpass the
capabilities of current light sources?

Nonlinear Optics of Plasmas and Laser-Plasma Interactions
This area investigates the interplay between coherent radiation and nonlinear states in
high-energy-density (HED) plasmas, nonlinear wave-particle interactions, multiple coexisting
instabilities, broadband radiation in plasma, and quantum phenomena. Important questions

5. High Energy and Nuclear Physics (HENP)
Program website: https://science.osti.gov/hep and https://science.osti.gov/np/
The mission of the High Energy and Nuclear Physics (HENP) program is to understand
how the universe works at its most fundamental level by discovering and investigating the
elementary constituents of matter and energy, probing the interactions between them, and
exploring the basic nature of space and time. HENP is the major sponsor of high energy
physics and nuclear physics research in the U.S., providing approximately 90% of the funding
allocated by the Federal government.
The scientific objectives and priorities for HENP recommended by the scientific
community are detailed in two recent long-range strategic plans, Pathways to Innovation and
Discovery in Particle Physics: Report of the 2023 Particle Physics Project Prioritization Panel
and A New Era of Discovery: The 2023 Long Range Plan for Nuclear Science.
Research in high energy physics is divided into four core areas covering three experimental
scientific frontiers and theory:
The Energy Frontier, where powerful accelerators are used to create new particles, reveal
their interactions, and investigate fundamental forces using highly sensitive collider-
based experimental detectors
The Intensity Frontier, where intense particle beams and highly sensitive detectors are
used to pursue alternate pathways to investigate fundamental forces and particle
interactions by studying events that occur rarely in nature, and to provide precision
measurements of these phenomena
The Cosmic Frontier, where data from the universe are used to probe fundamental
physics questions and offer new insight about the nature of dark matter, cosmic
acceleration in the forms of dark energy and inflation in the early universe, neutrino
properties, and other phenomena

(b) Experimental Research at the Intensity Frontier in High Energy Physics
This research area seeks to support precision studies that are sensitive to new physical

(c) Experimental Research at the Cosmic Frontier in High Energy Physics
This research area seeks to support precision studies using observations of the cosmos
and naturally occurring cosmic particles to understand the properties of fundamental particles
and fields. Priorities include the study of cosmic acceleration by studying the nature of dark
energy, planning the next-generation ground-based cosmic microwave background experiment to
explore the inflationary epoch, and using direct-detection experiments to search for dark matter
particles. Many of the experiments in the program also place constraints on neutrino masses.
Measurements using high-energy cosmic rays, gamma rays and other phenomena are included,
but at a lower priority.
HENP is looking for applications for physics research efforts supporting current
experiments in the Cosmic Frontier, as well as physics studies and pre-conceptual planning
directed towards specific future experiments being considered for the program. This research
area also provides undergraduate, graduate, and postdoctoral research training for the next
generation of scientists and equipment and computational support for physics research activities.

(i) Quantum Information Science for High Energy Physics Research

subprogram. This research primarily seeks to understand the internal structure of hadrons, the
dynamics of quarks within them, and to rigorously test Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD), the
theory describing the strong interaction. QCD posits that all observed nuclear particles,
collectively known as hadrons (such as the familiar protons and neutrons in atomic nuclei), are
formed from the profound interaction of quarks, antiquarks, and gluons. Although
computationally complex, QCD predicts the existence, interaction, and decay of these particles.
Key questions guiding research in hadron structure include:

How does experimental data affect QCD-Theory's predictions for the properties of

To address these questions, this part of the program aims to develop a comprehensive
understanding of the spatial, momentum, and angular momentum substructure of the nucleon. It
also strives to understand the mysteries of quark confinement, hadron excitations, and the origin
of mass while deepening our knowledge of the strong interaction within nuclei.
Experimental efforts employ various approaches to map the distribution of up, down, and
strange quarks; their antiquarks; and gluons inside protons and neutrons. These experiments also
clarify the critical role of gluons in confining quarks and antiquarks within hadrons, often using
polarized electron and proton beams to probe the effects of quark and gluon spins and nuclear
environmental influences. The subprogram further supports experimental searches for predicted
excited states and exotic hadrons, alongside studies of their production and decay.
The hadron part of the program primarily supports experimental research at its
cornerstone facility, the Continuous Electron Beam Accelerator Facility (CEBAF) at the Thomas
Jefferson National Accelerator Facility (TJNAF). Data analysis support also extends to other
significant facilities, such as the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) at Brookhaven National
Laboratory (BNL), the High Intensity Gamma Source (HIGS) at the Triangle Universities
Nuclear Laboratory (TUNL), and the future Electron-Ion Collider (EIC).
The studies of bulk, hot, or dense nuclear matter was formerly covered by the Heavy Ion
nuclear physics subprogram. The primary facilities in the world currently capable of exploring
the properties nuclear matter in these conditions have been the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider
(RHIC) at Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) and the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at the
European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN). The goal is to explore and understand
unique manifestations of QCD in this many-body environment and their influence on the
Universe's evolution. Important avenues of investigation are directed at resolving properties of
the quark-gluon plasma at different length scales and learning more about its physical
characteristics including its temperature, the energy loss mechanism for quarks and gluons
traversing the quark-gluon plasma, determining the speed of sound in the quark-gluon plasma,
measuring the effect of the chiral magnetic force, understanding how quarks fragment and
recombine to form hadronic matter (hadronization), and locating a possible critical point for the
transition between the quark-gluon plasma and normal matter. The high baryon-density region
of the phase diagram for nuclear matter continues to be explored. Experimental research is
carried out primarily using the data collected at the (now decommissioned) RHIC facility and the
LHC.

(m) Nuclear Structure and Nuclear Astrophysics
The Nuclear Structure and Nuclear Astrophysics subprogram focuses on high-impact
science with proton-rich and neutron-rich nuclei and searches for super-heavy elements and
nuclear processes that inform our understanding of stellar nucleosynthesis, neutron stars, and Big
Bang nucleosynthesis. Both frontiers are identified in A New Era of Discovery: The 2023 Long
Range Plan for Nuclear Science.
The subfield of nuclear structure addresses the underlying nature of atomic nuclei and the
limits of their existence and describes dynamical processes such as nuclear reactions and fission.
The goal is to develop a predictive understanding of nuclei and their interactions grounded in
fundamental QCD and electroweak theory; furthermore, this understanding must be based on
experimental data from a wide variety of nuclei.
Nuclear astrophysics addresses the role of nuclear physics in our universe. At the
interface of astrophysics and nuclear physics, this field is concerned with the impact of nuclear
processes on the evolution of the universe, the role of nuclear structure in influencing the
evolution of the cosmos, and the cosmogenic origin of elements that are the building blocks of
life. Nuclear astrophysics is a broad discipline that can identify new observational signatures
probing our universe, can identify the conditions at the very core of stars, and provide a record of
the violent history of the universe. To fully realize the discovery potential of this subfield,
interdisciplinary collaborations must be formed and strongly encouraged that include nuclear
experimentalists, theorists, and multi-messenger observers with a broad range of expertise.
Major goals of this subprogram are to develop a comprehensive description of nuclei
across the entire nuclear chart, including as-yet undiscovered superheavy nuclei, to utilize rare
isotope beams to reveal new nuclear phenomena and structures unlike those that are derived from
studies using stable ion beams, and to measure the cross sections of nuclear reactions that power
stars and spectacular stellar explosions and are responsible for the synthesis of the elements.

(n) Fundamental Symmetries
https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2280968/
This subprogram supports precision experiments to test the fundamental symmetries of
nature and discover new particles and forces. These experiments address some of the most
profound questions in physics today: What physical processes are responsible for the dominance
of matter over antimatter in our universe? What is the fundamental nature of the neutrino, and is
it its own antiparticle? What is the absolute mass of the neutrino? The research supported by
this subprogram seeks to provide answers to these questions by using the nucleus and its decays
as an observatory for new physics.
A major focus of this subprogram is the search for neutrinoless double beta decay
(0vBB). The observation of this hypothetical decay would demonstrate that the neutrino is its
own antiparticle (a Majorana fermion) and prove that lepton number conservation is violated.
This discovery would provide a key mechanism for explaining the cosmic matter-antimatter
asymmetry. The subprogram aligns with the priority within A New Era of Discovery: The 2023
Long Range Plan for Nuclear Science to implement a ton-scale 0vBB experiment.
The subprogram also supports experiments searching for permanent electric dipole
moments (EDMs) in neutrons and atoms. A non-zero EDM would, through a combination of
charge (C) and parity (P) violation, demonstrate time-reversal (T) symmetry violation, signaling
physics beyond the Standard Model. These low-energy, high-precision measurements provide
constraints on new CP-violating phases that are complementary to direct searches at high-energy
colliders.

(o) Nuclear Data
The Nuclear Data subprogram is multi-disciplinary with applications to energy, defense,
space, and medicine. Nuclear data underlies modeling and simulation software in nuclear
applications, so it is key for ensuring results are accurate. Working groups have been established
among researchers, as well as Federal programs, to help coordinate, prioritize, and fund research

data with its support of the U.S. Nuclear Data Program (USNDP) and the National Nuclear Data
Center (NNDC). This process combines historical and modern experiments, theory, and
modeling to publish best values for nuclear properties such as cross sections and decay data. The
extensive nuclear databases produced by this effort are an international resource consisting of
carefully organized scientific information gathered over 50 years of nuclear physics research
worldwide.

VI. Application Review Information
Standards for Application Evaluation
Applications that pass the initial review will be subjected to a formal merit review and
will be evaluated based on the criteria codified at 10 C.F.R. § 605.10(d) in accordance with the
guidance provided in the Office of Science Merit Review System for Financial Assistance.
Responsiveness Review
We will not review or consider the following concept papers and applications:
Project concepts or approaches identified as NOT of interest (see the Applications
Specifically Not of Interest section above)
Applicants and applications that do NOT meet the Eligibility Criteria in NOFO Parts 1
and 2
Review Criteria
1. Compliance Criteria
Prior to a comprehensive merit evaluation, DOE will perform an initial review in
accordance with 10 C.F.R. § 605.10(b) to determine that: (1) the applicant is eligible for the
award; (2) the information required by the NOFO, including LOIs or pre-applications, has been
submitted; (3) all mandatory requirements are satisfied; (4) the proposed project is responsive to
the objectives of the NOFO; and (5) the proposed project does not duplicate programmatic work.
Applications that fail to pass the initial review will not be forwarded for merit review and will be
eliminated from further consideration.
2. Technical Review Criteria
Applications will be subjected to scientific merit review (peer review) and will be
evaluated against the following criteria, listed in descending order of importance, as found in 10
C.F.R. § 605.10 (d), the Office of Science Financial Assistance Program Rule.
Scientific and/or Technical Merit of the Project
Appropriateness of the Proposed Method or Approach
Competency of Applicant's Personnel and Adequacy of Proposed Resources
Reasonableness and Appropriateness of the Proposed Budget
Appropriateness of the Data Management and Sharing Plan
External peer reviewers are selected regarding both their scientific expertise and the
absence of conflict-of-interest issues. Both Federal and non-Federal reviewers may be used, and

1. Government Rights in Data
The U.S. Government rights to data produced under an award or used in the performance
of the award varies according to the following classifications.
Normally, the government has unlimited rights in technical data created under a DOE
agreement, including the right to distribute to the public. Delivery or third-party licensing of
proprietary software or data developed solely at private expense (Limited Rights Data) will not
normally be required except as specifically negotiated in a particular agreement to satisfy DOE's
own needs or to ensure the commercialization of technology developed under a DOE agreement.
If software is specified for delivery to DOE, or if other special circumstances exist (e.g.,
DOE specifying "open-source" treatment of software), then the DOE Contracting Officer, after
negotiation with the recipient, may include special provisions in the award requiring the recipient
to obtain written approval of the DOE Contracting Officer prior to asserting copyright in the
software, modifying the retained Government license, or otherwise altering the copyright
provisions.
